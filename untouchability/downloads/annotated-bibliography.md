# Understanding Untouchability: annotated bibliography of reception

Compiled 1 November 2026. Coverage identified through English-language web searches, archived press clippings, author bibliographies, journal records, and book text/previews. This is a consolidated research bibliography, not a claim to have found every mention, particularly in Gujarati or inaccessible print archives.

## Reception and subsequent influence

Published in 2010 after initial press coverage in December 2009, *Understanding Untouchability* brought systematic evidence of caste exclusion into debates about Gujarat’s development. Its reception extended from newspaper reporting and scholarly discussion to a government-commissioned review, the wider history of Navsarjan’s advocacy, and a Supreme Court judgment fourteen years later. The sections below distinguish the report’s documented uses from disputed accounts of political motivation.

## The report in the Supreme Court

On 1 August 2024, the Supreme Court of India decided *State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh* (2024 INSC 562), upholding the constitutional permissibility of sub-classification within Scheduled Castes for reservations. Chief Justice D. Y. Chandrachud’s opinion used the report as empirical evidence that these communities are socially heterogeneous.

Paragraph 140 names the RFK Centre and Navsarjan, identifies the 1,589-village study, and discusses its findings on discrimination between Dalit subcastes. It describes exclusions from shared meals, drinking water, religious practices, and burial grounds. Footnote 166 cites *Understanding Untouchability*, pages 22–33. The passage appears under “Empirical evidence of heterogeneity,” following a discussion of A. M. Shah’s research in paragraph 139. [Read the judgment, paragraphs 139–140 (PDF page 43)](https://clpr.org.in/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/6.-The_State_of_Punjab_and_Ors_vs_Davinder_Singh_and_SC20240208241752101COM428239.pdf#page=43).

This is a specific and consequential use of the study: evidence assembled for local advocacy helped inform constitutional reasoning about inequality within historically disadvantaged communities. The citation documents that influence; it does not amount to judicial validation of every finding or of the survey’s entire methodology.

## Engagement

The tables distinguish substantive discussion from confirmed reference-list citations and institutional publicity. Summaries describe what the source says; endorsement, replication, and independent methodological validation are different things. Links are to originals where available, otherwise to the identified archive or reproduction. Some pages are intermittently inaccessible.

December 2009 articles report 1,655 villages and approximately 98,000 people represented. The final English report describes 1,589 villages and 5,462 respondents. These numbers should not be silently equated. Contemporary sources also alternate between 98 and 99 practices. ## Gujarat government–CEPT review and ensuing debate

The report’s findings challenged a public account of Gujarat’s development that left persistent caste exclusion out of view. After the original survey and the December 2009 newspaper series, the Gujarat government commissioned CEPT University—the Centre for Environmental Planning and Technology—to examine the findings. Contemporary reporting dates the commission to October 2010. In 2018, Social Justice and Empowerment Minister Ishwar Parmar told the Legislative Assembly that the government had considered Navsarjan’s report one-sided and anti-government and had asked CEPT to conduct a fact-finding survey. He said CEPT’s report was received in April 2013. [The government’s explanation, reported by The New Indian Express](https://www.newindianexpress.com/amp/story/india/2018/Mar/07/gujarat-government-refuses-to-table-survey-report-on-untouchability-1783126.html).

The debate mattered because it concerned whose evidence would guide recognition of untouchability and the state’s response. CEPT’s five-village study drew criticism for treating discrimination as a matter of perception and for concentrating on socioeconomic conditions rather than directly examining exclusion. Navsarjan returned to the five villages and reported continuing restrictions. Questions about access to the commissioned report, pursued through Right to Information requests, made government transparency part of the controversy. [Contemporary coverage of the review](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/city/rajkot/untouchability-remains-untouched-in-gujarat-report/articleshow/21155467.cms) · [Coverage of Navsarjan’s resurvey](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/city/rajkot/Untouchability-still-rampant-in-villages-Navsarjan-report/articleshow/21890392.cms).

The positions were not uniform. In July 2013, CEPT lead researcher R. Parthasarathy publicly described Navsarjan’s study as “comprehensive and excellent” and his own study as complementary. Sociologist Ghanshyam Shah’s detailed critique supported Navsarjan’s approach, but he disclosed membership of its board. These distinctions matter: government criticism, CEPT researchers’ statements, and subsequent scholarly assessments should not be treated as a single position. ## Navsarjan’s funding crisis and continuing advocacy

The dispute over the report forms part of a longer history of Navsarjan’s advocacy and its relationship with government. On 15 December 2016, the Union Ministry of Home Affairs cancelled the Trust’s registration under the Foreign Contribution Regulation Act (FCRA), preventing it from receiving overseas donations. Contemporary reporting describes foreign funding as central to its operations. Navsarjan then sought resignations from its 80 employees and announced that three primary schools would close. Martin Macwan attributed the action to its role in the protests following the July 2016 Una flogging. Ministry officials denied that explanation and cited adverse intelligence reports. [The Indian Express, 23 December 2016](https://indianexpress.com/article/cities/ahmedabad/fcra-licence-cancelled-ngo-navsarjan-lays-off-all-staffers-4440969/).

The cancellation severely disrupted Navsarjan’s staffing and programmes. It should not, however, be described as a proven consequence of this report or as a permanent closure of the organization. In an August 2026 account, Macwan described Navsarjan and Dalit Shakti Kendra continuing their work despite the loss of FCRA registration. The relationship to the report is therefore contextual: both episodes belong to the history of challenges to Navsarjan’s anti-discrimination advocacy, while a direct causal link between publication and the funding decision remains unestablished by the sources cited here. [Macwan’s later account in India Development Review](https://idronline.org/article/perspectives/what-is-civil-societys-purpose/).
